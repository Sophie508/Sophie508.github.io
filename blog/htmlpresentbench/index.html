The agent finished.
Can you understand
what it did?

A useful report does more than contain an answer. It tells you what happened, makes the evidence readable, and helps you find what you came for.

HTMLPresentBench evaluates that handoff: from a completed agent execution to a report for people. We hold the execution fixed and ask how well different reports communicate it.

01 / The problem

You want one number, not the whole log.

Imagine opening an agent’s stock report to find the closing price on June 4. The agent has already retrieved it. You should not have to read every tool call to find the number—or search again to learn where it came from.

The report can fail you in three different ways: give the wrong price, make the price unreadable, or bury it behind an unclear link. These are different failures, even when they happen on the same page.

02 / Three dimensions

Correct. Readable. Easy to reach.

Content checks meaning. Visual Quality checks the rendered page. Information Access checks the reader’s route to an answer. Try changing one part of this report and watch which problem appears.

The first two dimensions inspect what the report says and shows. The third needs one more ingredient: what does this reader want to know?

03 / Follow the question

A number leads to another question.

You found CNY 12.80. Now you want its source, or to check whether it is a closing price rather than an intraday high. A reader-need tree connects these questions. It is prepared before any candidate report is inspected.

Findability asks whether the answer has a clear entry point. Continuity asks whether a reader can follow an answer to related details. A descriptive link, an adjacent explanation, or a well-labeled expandable section can all help.

The reference defines the questions; the report supplies the routes. A source link next to the price helps connect the two answers. An unexplained log several screens away leaves the reader searching.

What exactly do the nodes and edges represent?

Each need node contains an ID, question text, and children. The root represents the overall task and is mapped to the page entry. The tree has no prescribed importance weights or preferred presentation formats. Findability assesses support for each answer; Continuity assesses the parent–child relations using explicit pointers, weaker cues, and distance measured from the rendered layout. Those judgments belong to the report being evaluated, not to fixed edge weights in the reference tree. See Appendix D.3–D.4.

Recovering a fact from HTML source is not enough: the reader needs a visible answer or a usable route to it. These checks assess that support; they do not directly measure reading speed or comprehension.

04 / The evaluation

Same work. Different reports.

Every reporting method receives the same completed trajectory and supporting files. It does not redo the task. Reference answers and post-hoc correctness judgments are excluded from generation inputs; feedback observed during execution is retained.

We then evaluate the reports against a shared reference. The reference describes the work and the reader’s questions, without prescribing a single correct webpage.

What work do the reports describe?

The study covers 60 trajectories across 13 domains: 30 from public datasets and 30 collected for the study. They span code repair, data analysis, desktop tasks, tutoring, and more. Explore the sources below.

How is this different from existing artifact evaluation?

Design2Code studies reconstruction from screenshots; WebGen-Bench studies building working websites; ArtifactsBench and PresentBench assess visual artifacts and slides. Research-report benchmarks also evaluate information and presentation. Here, the input is an already completed agent trajectory—including mistakes, observations, and available outputs—and the report must communicate that specific execution. The contribution is a trajectory-grounded, reusable assessment with separate content, visual, and reader-access checks. It does not claim to invent those design principles. 05 / What the experiments reveal

Easier to navigate. Not better at everything.

Across the eleven generators, Direct HTML improves average Access over Markdown, from 49.5 to 53.6. HTML Anything reaches 61.5. Yet Markdown has the highest Visual score for every model, with an average of 97.7: its renderer supplies reliable typography and layout.

Adding design guidance shifts the balance again. Relative to Direct HTML, HTML Anything improves Access for ten of eleven models, but lowers Content for all eleven and Visual quality for ten. The two guided HTML methods—Frontend Design and HTML Anything—are shown alongside Markdown and Direct HTML below. Compare the three dimensions separately.

Improvement can stop short of access.

Under Direct HTML, GPT-6-Astra improves over GPT-5.6-Sol in Content and Visual quality, but Access is lower: 53.5 versus 57.3. Claude Fable 5.1 gains 10.1 points in Content and 10.7 in Visual quality over Opus 4.8, while Access gains only 1.6. A clearer page still needs a deliberate path from its conclusion to its evidence.

A better shortcut can omit a necessary qualification.

In another example, a prominent “Done: true” card makes the end state easier to locate but omits the accompanying reward of 0. The report becomes easier to navigate while giving a less complete account of the outcome. The paper also documents overlapping status stamps, broken images, low contrast, and mobile clipping. A page loading successfully is only the beginning of visual validation. Figures 6–9 ↗

See a real case: the same code-repair run, three different reports

A SAS parser task ends with a reproduction file, but no parser fix. Three reports describe that same execution: one buries verification on its third screen, another puts it in an opening banner, and a third links the verdict to the submitted patch.

Reporting a failed execution accurately can be good reporting. Task success and presentation quality are separate questions.

06 / Check against people

Does the evaluator agree with readers?

Eighteen participants each review ten sets of three interactive reports. Within a set, the trajectory and reporting model are fixed; the generation method varies. Model and method names are hidden. Participants judge understandability and select the report in which key answers and evidence are easiest to find.

The full pipeline is compared with a simpler judge using the same evaluator model. The results below measure agreement with human orderings—not task success, not reader comprehension accuracy, and not a direct reading-time improvement.

Explore a teaching interaction: when are the bounds tight enough?

Can an interaction explain, rather than just navigate?

The paper’s tutoring case uses buttons and a slider to connect numerical bounds to a plot. This independent example illustrates the idea: if x is the positive root of x² = 2, what is ⌊x³⌋?

The slider earns its place by answering a question: why is the first bound insufficient, and what changes when it gets tighter? This is the opportunity the qualitative examples suggest. The study does not establish that every interaction improves comprehension, or that more controls produce a better report.

What this supports—and what remains open.

A wrong price, an unreadable price, and a buried price call for different fixes. Separating the dimensions makes these problems inspectable, while shared references support controlled comparisons of the same work.

The benchmark is still a sample of 60 trajectories, the judge still makes interpretive decisions, and the human study covers only part of the criteria. Information-access scores are proxies for reader support, not direct measurements of cognitive load. Interactive teaching is a promising qualitative observation that deserves targeted user studies.
