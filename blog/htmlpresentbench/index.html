PresentBench.The work is done.
Can a reader understand it?

An agent finishes a task. Its report contains the answer, the evidence, and the caveats. But can you actually find them?

HTMLPresentBench evaluates the handoff from an agent’s completed execution to a report for people. It asks three separate questions: is the content faithful, is it readable, and is the information easy to reach?

01 / The handoff

The answer can be present
and still be hard to find.

Consider a code-repair task: a parser—the code that reads SAS statements—needs to handle a particular syntax. The agent submits a small reproduction file, but does not change the parser. A reader’s first question is simple: was the issue fixed?

In the paper’s example, three reports describe the same run. One places the outcome in a metadata table and verification on its third screen. Another puts both in an opening banner. A third adds routes to the verdict, execution steps, and patch. The content stays similar; the reading experience changes.

That is the distinction this benchmark studies. Reporting a failed execution accurately can be good reporting. Completing a task successfully does not guarantee a clear report. We hold the execution fixed so we can examine the presentation itself.

How is this different from existing artifact evaluation?

Design2Code studies reconstruction from screenshots; WebGen-Bench studies building working websites; ArtifactsBench and PresentBench assess visual artifacts and slides. Research-report benchmarks also evaluate information and presentation. Here, the input is an already completed agent trajectory—including mistakes, observations, and available outputs—and the report must communicate that specific execution. The contribution is a trajectory-grounded, reusable assessment with separate content, visual, and reader-access checks. It does not claim to invent those design principles. 02 / Three different questions

Good content. Clear rendering.
A useful path through both.

A page can be beautiful and wrong. It can be accurate and unreadable. It can even be accurate and readable while burying the answer. One overall impression cannot tell these failures apart.

The framework separates three axes. Try changing one aspect of this miniature report, then inspect the relevant checks.

Content asks whether the report preserves the right meaning. Visual quality asks whether the rendered elements can be read. Information access asks whether the page helps a reader reach the information they need. That last question requires a reference for what the reader is looking for.

03 / Start with a reader’s question

“What happened?”
is only the first question.

Once readers find the outcome, they may ask what changed, why the task stopped, and what evidence supports the conclusion. The framework represents these relationships as a reader-need tree: questions and follow-up questions, rather than a required page layout.

Findability asks whether the answer has a clear entry point. Continuity asks whether a reader can follow an answer to related details. A descriptive link, an adjacent explanation, or a well-labeled expandable section can all help.

This avoids judging a page only by what an LLM can recover from its source. A model may connect two facts far apart in extracted text. A person still needs a visible cue and a usable route between them. The checks assess that support; they do not directly measure reading speed or comprehension.

04 / An inspectable evaluation

Fix the work.
Vary the report.

Every reporting method receives the same completed trajectory and supporting files, without redoing the task. Reference answers and post-hoc correctness judgments are excluded from generation inputs; feedback observed during execution is retained.

Evaluation then separates reusable task references from evidence prepared for each report. This is how diverse designs can be compared without specifying a single “correct” webpage.

A screenshot shows what is visible, but misses some collapsed content. Source code contains information that may be clipped or unreadable in the browser. The pipeline combines a semantic map with rendered views and captured interaction states. The paper uses desktop (1440 × 1000) and mobile (390 × 844) rendering.

05 / What the experiments reveal

Easier to navigate
does not mean better at everything.

Across the eleven generators, Direct HTML improves average Access over Markdown, from 49.5 to 53.6. HTML Anything reaches 61.5. Yet Markdown has the highest Visual score for every model, with an average of 97.7: its renderer supplies reliable typography and layout.

Adding design guidance shifts the balance again. Relative to Direct HTML, HTML Anything improves Access for ten of eleven models, but lowers Content for all eleven and Visual quality for ten. Explore the three axes together instead of collapsing them into a single winner.

Improvement can stop short of access.

Under Direct HTML, GPT-6-Astra improves over GPT-5.6-Sol in Content and Visual quality, but Access is lower: 53.5 versus 57.3. Claude Fable 5.1 gains 10.1 points in Content and 10.7 in Visual quality over Opus 4.8, while Access gains only 1.6. A clearer page still needs a deliberate path from its conclusion to its evidence.

A better shortcut can omit a necessary qualification.

In another example, a prominent “Done: true” card makes the end state easier to locate but omits the accompanying reward of 0. The report becomes easier to navigate while giving a less complete account of the outcome. The paper also documents overlapping status stamps, broken images, low contrast, and mobile clipping. 06 / Beyond navigation

An interaction should
resolve a reader’s difficulty.

The most interesting examples do more than help readers jump between sections. In the paper’s tutoring case, a student has bounds on a root but cannot determine an integer value. Buttons compare loose and tightened bounds; a slider connects changing numbers to a live plot.

Try the same teaching idea in a smaller, independent example. Let x be the positive root of x² = 2. What is ⌊x³⌋—the greatest integer no larger than x³?

The slider earns its place by answering a question: why is the first bound insufficient, and what changes when it gets tighter? This is the opportunity the qualitative examples suggest. The study does not establish that every interaction improves comprehension, or that more controls produce a better report.

07 / Check the evaluator, too

Does the assessment
agree with readers?

Eighteen participants each review ten sets of three interactive reports. Within a set, the trajectory and reporting model are fixed; the generation method varies. Model and method names are hidden. Participants judge understandability and select the report in which key answers and evidence are easiest to find.

The full pipeline is compared with a simpler judge using the same evaluator model. The results below measure agreement with human orderings—not task success, not reader comprehension accuracy, and not a direct reading-time improvement.

What this supports—and what remains open.

The framework makes reporting quality more inspectable: a missing qualification, an unreadable chart, and a disconnected evidence trail become different diagnoses. Its reusable references support controlled comparisons across reports of the same execution.

The benchmark is still a sample of 60 trajectories, the judge still makes interpretive decisions, and the human study covers only part of the criteria. Information-access scores are proxies for reader support, not direct measurements of cognitive load. Interactive teaching is a promising qualitative observation that deserves targeted user studies.

The goal is not more interface.
It is less work between a result
and a reader’s understanding.
